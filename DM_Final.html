The first dataset about pollution per counties in the United States, was published by The Environmental Protection agency and more precisely measured by the Air Quality System (AQS). It’s measure system is based on a monitor located in most counties which measures different pollution levels and metrics (ozone, carbone monoxyde …). Instead of a precise device a monitor represent a place where a parameter is being measure at a given site. In this coument you have

The second element of our study is a group of datasets representing different measures of socio-economic features in all counties of the US. These measures were collected by the American community survey and more precisely by the Census Bureau and are published every year and is of the most reliable information source about societey characteristics in America.

Important Variables

Median household income in the past 12 months (inflation adjusted dollars) : This is a quite explicit variable, it’s a very straightforward way to measure wealth in each county and we picked it over mean because it is not skewed by high incomes. One limit is that there are margins of error because these are survey data (note that the margins of error rarely exceed 10 000usd for annual income).

Employment rate : This is a variable we’re going to compute manually (dividing the working population over the total population of the county), it’s another very useful measure and we could observe if there exists higher or lower exposition to pollution when the county is more or less employed. This also interesting because maybe some counties are more industrial and have higher employment rate but also higher pollution exposition.

Ozone measured with arithmetic mean of 8hour average with 2015 as reference (most recent data we have), we chose this variable to partly represent pollution in counties because first of all it’s the measured on the most monitors so it allows us to really have a precise figure. But also it’s one of the most impacting particle for health.

cleaning_ACS <- function(df){
  names(df)[2:ncol(df)] <- as.character(df[1, 2:ncol(df)])
  df <- df[-1, ] #explicit title is in line 1
  df <- df[, -ncol(df)] #always full of NA
  df[3:ncol(df)] <- lapply(df[3:ncol(df)], as.integer) #integer type for every column but 1 & 2
  df
}
